\paragraph{Automated debates.}
\Cref{tab:auto} reports the judge's scores. \sys{} scored higher than Deep Research on stance consistency, logical coherence, evidence integration, rebuttal specificity, and persuasiveness, and lower on fluency. The transcripts suggest a common explanation across the three motions. In the theism debate, \sys{}'s Pro case began from the claim that moral objectivity requires a transcendent foundation and supported it with sub-claims that each carried evidence, such as rates of volunteering in religious populations. Deep Research's Con case relied on the Euthyphro dilemma and repeated it across turns with little new evidence. With the sides reversed, \sys{} argued the Con case through meta-ethical consistency, cross-cultural variation in moral codes, and secular moral psychology, while Deep Research's Pro turns restated earlier points. In the semiconductor debate, \sys{} divided its Pro case into measurable claims about the cost of capital under the subsidies, the integration of research with fabrication, the resilience of supply chains, and projected yields. Its Con case rested on quantified dependencies, including a projected plateau in the U.S.\ share of fabrication capacity near 14\% by 2032. Deep Research's cases on both sides remained qualitative. In the creativity debate, \sys{} separated an empirical branch, which cited benchmarks of divergent thinking, from a conceptual branch, which defined creativity as the combination of novelty and usefulness. Deep Research argued from metaphysical objections without an operational definition. Across the six transcripts, \sys{} made roughly twice as many claims linked to evidence. Each of these differences follows from the structure of the pipeline, which forces every sub-claim to be answered and supported before the case is assembled. The one measure on which Deep Research scored higher is fluency. Its turns read more smoothly, whereas \sys{}'s dense citation of sub-claims interrupts the flow of the prose.

\paragraph{Human assessment of reports.}
\Cref{tab:human} reports the rater's scores. \sys{} had the highest supported-claims rate (\humSupportedDC{} against \humSupportedOAI{} for Deep Research and \humSupportedStorm{} for STORM), used the largest share of its cited sources (\humUtilDC{} against \humUtilOAI{} and \humUtilStorm{}), and received the highest score for logical connection (\humLogicDC{} against \humLogicOAI{} and \humLogicStorm{}). It held a firm position in \humStanceDC{} of 10 reports, whereas Deep Research did so in \humStanceOAI{} and STORM in \humStanceStorm{}. The rater's notes describe how these differences appear in the text. On the motion concerning a moral duty to strangers, \sys{} built a layered argument that linked empathy to social trust, Deep Research gave a balanced account that reached no decision, and STORM described the positions without connecting them causally. On the Federal Reserve motion, \sys{} argued through a chain from inflation control to wage stability, while Deep Research summarized both effects without relating them. On the motion concerning investment in foundation models, \sys{} produced a conditional argument, holding that foundation models concentrate capital efficiency unless saturation reduces marginal returns, which neither baseline matched.

Two of these differences have plausible sources in the design of the pipeline. The higher supported-claims rate is expected because every leaf answer is written from search results with citation markers and every parent is composed only from its children, which leaves an uncited claim little route into the report. This measure, however, records whether a claim carries a citation and not whether the citation is correct, a question that \Cref{sec:citations} addresses directly. The higher rate of firm positions follows in part from the report prompt, which instructs the model to argue for the root answer, and \Cref{sec:discussion} returns to this point.
