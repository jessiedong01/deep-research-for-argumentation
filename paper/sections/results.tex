\Cref{tab:auto} shows the automated debate scores. \sys{} scores higher than Deep Research on stance consistency, logical coherence, evidence integration, rebuttal specificity, and persuasiveness. Deep Research scores higher on fluency. The transcripts indicate a more coherent pattern across the three motions. For theism, \sys{}'s Pro case starts from the premise that moral objectivity requires a transcendent anchor, which is justified through sub-claims supported by evidence such as differences in volunteering rates among religious groups. By contrast, Deep Research's Con case invokes the Euthyphro dilemma and repeats it with little additional evidence.

For the reversed sides, \sys{}'s Con case appeals to meta-ethical consistency, cross-cultural moral variation, and secular moral psychology. Deep Research's Pro arguments simply reiterate previous points. On the semiconductor motion, \sys{}'s Pro case divides the case into quantifiable claims about capital costs with subsidies, integration of research and fabrication, supply chain resilience, and expected yields. The Con case cites quantified dependencies, such as a plateau in the U.S.\ share of fabrication capacity around 14\% by 2032. Deep Research's arguments for both sides are qualitative.

For creativity, \sys{} separates empirical sub-claims comparing divergent thinking benchmarks from the conceptual claim that creativity is novelty plus usefulness. Deep Research argues from metaphysical considerations without an operational definition. Across all six transcripts, \sys{} produces roughly twice as many sub-claims connected to evidence. All of these differences are a consequence of the pipeline structure, which requires every sub-claim to be answered and supported before the case is composed. The only measure that Deep Research scores higher than \sys{} is the fluency score. Deep Research's arguments read more smoothly because \sys{}'s heavy reliance on sub-claim citations disrupts the prose flow.

\Cref{tab:human} shows the human assessment scores. \sys{} has the highest supported-claims rate (\humSupportedDC{} vs.\ \humSupportedOAI{} for Deep Research and \humSupportedStorm{} for STORM) and the highest fraction of cited sources utilized (\humUtilDC{} vs.\ \humUtilOAI{} for Deep Research and \humUtilStorm{} for STORM). It also has the highest average score for logical connection (\humLogicDC{} vs.\ \humLogicOAI{} for Deep Research and \humLogicStorm{} for STORM). \sys{} maintains a firm position in \humStanceDC{} out of 10 reports, compared to \humStanceOAI{} report for Deep Research and \humStanceStorm{} reports for STORM. The human rater's written comments provide concrete examples of these scoring differences. For the motion on a moral obligation to strangers, \sys{} constructs a multi-layered argument connecting empathy to social trust. Deep Research provides a balanced account that fails to reach a conclusion. STORM outlines positions without establishing causal links.

For the Federal Reserve motion, \sys{} builds a chain from inflation control to wage stability, whereas Deep Research mentions both effects without connecting them. For the foundation model investment motion, \sys{}'s argument is conditional: foundation models concentrate capital efficiency, unless saturation dampens marginal returns. Both baseline models lack this conditional structure.

We have plausible explanations for the two differences. More reported claims are supported because every leaf answer has its own citations, while all parent contributions are derived from its children, giving little opportunity for uncited claims. Note that the measure only checks whether a claim has a citation in it, not whether the citation is accurate, which we discuss in \Cref{sec:citations}. Similarly, a higher proportion of firm positions partly reflects the report prompt, which instructs the model to defend the root answer (we return to this in \Cref{sec:discussion}).
