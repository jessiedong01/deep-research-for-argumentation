\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate \sys{} and compare it to OpenAI Deep Research \citep{openai2025deepresearch} and STORM \citep{shao2024storm} using two evaluation methods: (1) an automated debate, where an LLM scores a debate between \sys{} and Deep Research, and (2) a blind human evaluation of final reports from all three systems. The evaluations are small in scale as detailed in \Cref{sec:discussion}.

We select three debate motions that require different types of reasoning: moral (a moral debate on whether moral systems rooted in theism are preferable to those not rooted in theism), economic and technical (an economic and technical debate on whether the CHIPS and Science Act will enable U.S.\ semiconductor manufacturers to outperform global competitors over the next decade), and conceptual (a conceptual debate on whether artificial intelligence can be creative in the way humans are). Each debate is run twice, with each system (\sys{} and Deep Research) alternating between the Pro and Con sides in each debate. Thus, we have a total of 6 transcripts and control for position bias.

To conduct each debate, each system independently researches its assigned motion. \sys{} stages its arguments based on the nodes of the DAG it builds, while Deep Research uses its standard retrieval and synthesis pipeline. We use GPT-5-mini to stage the debate. Given the research produced by the two systems for each motion, GPT-5-mini debates back and forth as each side would. The debate consists of alternating sides, with 200--300 words each turn, based on the side's own research and the opponent's last turn.

GPT-4o-mini scores the debate transcripts on stance consistency (the fraction of turns that maintain the assigned side), logical coherence, integration of evidence, specificity of rebuttal, persuasiveness, and fluency. Each of the other categories is scored on a 1--10 scale, following DeepResearch Bench (unpublished project by Abhinav Chinta, Charles Ding).

Reports on ten motions were assessed by a human reader. These were chosen because they corresponded to motions in which a synthesis of evidence was necessary, and because the report's author had to take a position, rather than simply state facts. The motions concerned ethics, economics, policy, and technology, and are listed in \Cref{tab:reports}. Each of the three AI systems wrote a report on each motion, meaning there were 30 reports in total. All of the reports were scored on the same rubric by one of the authors, who was blind to which system wrote each report. The rubric was composed of four measures: the fraction of claims that carry a citation, which we refer to as the supported-claims rate; the fraction of cited sources actually utilized in the argument; the quality of logical connection, on a scale from 1 to 10; and whether the report maintains a firm position throughout.

The rubric also included scores for evidence relevance and originality, but these were at the same level for each of the three systems, and can not be reconstructed. Therefore, these measures were excluded from the analysis. Furthermore, there was only one rater, so score agreement statistics are not applicable. The scores are means over ten reports, and confidence intervals are not reported.

The repository includes all the \sys{} and STORM reports on the ten motions, plus the prompts, pipeline, and single logged run. Deep Research reports and transcripts were not retained. Therefore, these results and discussions in \Cref{sec:analysis,sec:citations} concern the twenty reports and logged run that were retained.
