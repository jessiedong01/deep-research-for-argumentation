\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deep research systems automate answering questions by asking multiple rounds of search queries. Each round involves reading search results and deciding if more queries are needed. The system eventually writes a report citing the page in which each fact appears. Two of the most well-known systems are OpenAI Deep Research \citep{openai2025deepresearch}, released in February 2025, and STORM \citep{shao2024storm}, from Stanford OVAL.

OpenAI Deep Research and STORM both produce summaries of information rather than taking a position on the information they encountered. For example, on the question ``Should the Federal Reserve keep interest rates high?'', the output is a set of evidence from which a reader can draw their own conclusions. While this is great for a starting point, many professional contexts require a final conclusion. A lawyer writing a brief makes a case for their client's side, and an analyst provides a recommendation to buy or sell an investment. In such contexts, the reader expects the writer to take a side and to provide claims with supporting evidence.

The distinction between a survey and an argument affects how research should be structured. A survey can be built incrementally, with each search round informing the next. The content of the report is ultimately decided by the search results. In contrast, an argument has a defined structure before searching. A claim has a few sub-claims, each of which must be supported by retrievable evidence. Research to generate an argument can be planned in advance by breaking down the argument into narrow sub-questions, which can be answered with search and combined together in argument order.

Our system, which we abbreviate \sys{} (combining ``deep research'' and ``decomposition''), uses the following pipeline. When given a question, a language model produces a directed acyclic graph of sub-questions, with instructions on how to combine the answers of child sub-questions into the parent node's. The leaf nodes are solved with retrieval-augmented generation, and the parent nodes are composed from their children in topological order. If the composed answer is incomplete, the system adds a new subtree of sub-questions below. The final graph is converted to an outline and to the actual report arguing for the root node's answer. \Cref{fig:pipeline} illustrates.

We evaluate this pipeline against OpenAI Deep Research and STORM, showing results from six automated debates and blind rater evaluation of 10 reports. Our pipeline receives the highest scores for argument quality and takes a side far more often than the baselines. A secondary analysis of the reports we retained, which does not use a rater, supports the finding that our pipeline takes a side more often, but qualifies the other two results. The reports have dense citations but few unique sources, the conclusions are often more modest than the titles, and an automated test for attribution indicates that when a fact is cited, the citation rarely points to the claim's original source. This problem is caused by a flaw in how our pipeline manages citations during composition, a flaw that can affect any system that stages text composition with citations.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. \S\ref{sec:problem} defines the task and highlights the challenge of retaining citations after composition; \S\ref{sec:method} describes the pipeline; \S\ref{sec:experiments} and \S\ref{sec:results} present the initial evaluation and its limitations; \S\ref{sec:analysis} analyzes the contents of the reports; \S\ref{sec:citations} describes an attribution test and shows how it identifies the flaw in our pipeline; \S\ref{sec:discussion} explains what the results are and are not evidence for.
