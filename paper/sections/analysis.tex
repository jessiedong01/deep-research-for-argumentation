\section{Analysis of the Reports}
\label{sec:analysis}

The scores in \Cref{tab:human} come from a single rater, so they are vulnerable to that rater's judgment. The twenty retained reports, ten from \sys{} and ten from STORM on the same motions, allow a second assessment that does not depend on any rater. Each report is measured in the same way, and the two systems are compared motion by motion, which controls for differences in difficulty between motions. Because the Deep Research reports were not retained, this section compares \sys{} with STORM only. All differences are medians of the paired per-motion differences, reported with 95\% bootstrap intervals over motions \citep{efron1993bootstrap} and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests \citep{wilcoxon1945}. With ten pairs, the smallest attainable two-sided $p$-value is 0.002. Bibliographies, headings, and markup are removed before counting.

\paragraph{Stance.}
If the rater's judgment of stance is correct, it should be visible in the language of the reports. Hedges are words such as \emph{may}, \emph{could}, and \emph{suggests} that withhold commitment \citep{hyland2005metadiscourse}, and commitment markers are words such as \emph{must}, \emph{demonstrates}, and \emph{therefore} that assert it. \sys{}'s reports use fewer hedges than STORM's, \txHedgeDc{} per 1{,}000 words against \txHedgeSt{}, and the difference holds on nine of ten motions ($p = \txHedgeP$). They also use more commitment markers, \txCommitDc{} per 1{,}000 words against \txCommitSt{}, on all ten motions ($p = \txCommitP$). Contrast markers such as \emph{however} and \emph{on the other hand} occur at similar rates in both systems (\txContrastDc{} and \txContrastSt{}, $p = \txContrastP$). \sys{} therefore acknowledges opposing views about as often as STORM does. It differs in committing to a position after doing so.

The commitment is strongest at the beginning of a report and weaker at its end. \Cref{tab:stance} records, for each \sys{} report, the side of the motion it takes, whether its title states a claim, and how its final paragraph concludes. Of the ten titles, \labTitleClaim{} state a claim, as the report prompt requires, whereas every STORM report opens with a lead section that frames the question as a debate. The final paragraphs are less committed. Of the ten, \labEndingFirm{} end firmly, \labEndingQualified{} qualify the claim of the title, \labEndingConditional{} make it conditional, and \labEndingCompromise{} replaces a yes-or-no answer with a compromise. For example, the creativity report concludes that human creativity remains irreducible ``but only provisionally,'' and the theism report concludes that the preference for theistic morality ``depends fundamentally on one's valuation'' of transcendent truth. One report also argues for the side opposite to the motion as worded, holding that application-layer startups will outperform developers of foundation models. A plausible explanation is that the stance instruction controls the title and executive summary, which are written first, whereas the conclusion is written last from a graph whose branches cover both sides of the question, so the balanced material reappears at the end.

\paragraph{Connection.}
The rater scored \sys{} higher on logical connection, and the original assessment attributed this to causal chains between sub-claims. Causal connectives such as \emph{because}, \emph{leads to}, and \emph{as a result} provide a rough lexical check of this explanation. Their rate does not differ reliably between the systems, at \txCausalDc{} per 1{,}000 words against \txCausalSt{}, with \sys{} higher on five of ten motions ($p = \txCausalP$). A count of connectives captures only part of what makes an argument coherent, so this result does not contradict the rater, but it provides no independent support for the explanation either.

\paragraph{Length and sourcing.}
\sys{}'s reports are shorter than STORM's on every motion, with a median of \txWordsDc{} words of body text against \txWordsSt{}. They cite more densely, at \txCiteDensityDc{} citations per 100 words against \txCiteDensitySt{} ($p = \txCiteDensityP$), but they draw on far fewer sources. The body of a \sys{} report cites a median of \txSourcesDc{} distinct sources, compared with \txSourcesSt{} for STORM, and each source is cited \txCitesPerSourceDc{} times on average against \txCitesPerSourceSt{}. Despite this density, a smaller share of \sys{}'s sentences carry any citation (\txCitedShareDc{} against \txCitedShareSt{}, lower on every motion). The two observations are consistent because \sys{} concentrates its citations in bullet points of evidence and leaves the connecting prose uncited. The bibliographies show the same concentration from the other direction. Each \sys{} report lists a median of \bibMedDc{} retrieved sources (range \bibMinDc{} to \bibMaxDc) and separates them into cited and uncited sources, but the body cites only \atUsedMin{} to \atUsedMax{} of them, a median of \atUsedShare\%. Most of the evidence that the pipeline retrieves never reaches the report.

\paragraph{Sources.}
\Cref{tab:sources} classifies the \srcN{} sources in \sys{}'s bibliographies by domain. Government sites account for \srcShareGovernment\% of them, academic publishers and university sites for \srcShareAcademic\%, think tanks and other non-profit organizations for \srcShareThinkTankOr\%, and company sites, blogs, and other pages for the largest share, \srcShareCompanyBlogOrOther\%. News sites are almost absent. Of the \linkChecked{} links, \linkOkPct\% still resolved when checked in October 2026, eleven months after the reports were written, \linkBlockedPct\% refused automated requests, and \linkDeadPct\% no longer existed or returned errors. The STORM exports contain citation numbers but no URLs, so the same check cannot be applied to STORM.

\paragraph{Structure.}
The two systems organize their reports in different ways. STORM writes in the form of an encyclopedia article, with a lead section that frames the question, a background section, and sections for the arguments on each side, and it often closes on the opposing case. \sys{} writes in the form of a brief, with a claim as the title, an executive summary that states the answer, sections that follow the branches of the graph, and a conclusion. On the motion concerning the African Continental Free Trade Area, STORM opens by describing the agreement and the history of its ratification, while \sys{} opens by stating that the evidence ``overwhelmingly supports'' the conclusion that the benefits outweigh the harms. On the motion concerning a moral duty to strangers, STORM ends with critics who find an equal obligation to strangers unrealistic, and \sys{} ends with a synthesis in which ``impartiality and partiality are not opposites,'' a softer position than its title asserts.
